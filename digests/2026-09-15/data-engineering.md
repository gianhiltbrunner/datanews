# Practitioners trade notes on enforcing data contracts across team boundaries

A thin Sunday for data engineering: no releases, and the day's discussion was driven by r/dataengineering rather than the newsletters. The one substantive write-up is a back-to-basics piece on threading, while the liveliest thread asks how to make cross-team data contracts enforceable without inventing a heavyweight process.

## Top stories
- **[data contracts between upstream and downstream teams](https://www.reddit.com/r/dataengineering/comments/1wg1up2/data_contracts_between_upstream_and_downstream/)** — r/dataengineering (top today). A Databricks user asks how to handle contracts for tables, views and table functions owned by other teams: whether to define a source spec yourself and check it on every run, what to demand from producers, and who owns a contract when several consumers depend on the same artifact. *Why it matters:* contract tooling is maturing faster than the ownership model around it, and that gap is where most teams stall.
- **[Back to the Basics. Threads.](https://dataengineeringcentral.substack.com/p/back-to-the-basics-threads)** — Data Engineering Central. The second instalment of a fundamentals series, following an earlier post on recursion in SQL and Rust, this time working through threads. *Why it matters:* a reminder that concurrency behaviour still decides pipeline throughput, however much of the code an agent writes.
- **[Pending job offer - devil you know vs devil you don't know?](https://www.reddit.com/r/dataengineering/comments/1wg6pdx/pending_job_offer_devil_you_know_vs_devil_you/)** — r/dataengineering (top today). An engineer three years into an on-prem MSSQL/SSIS to Snowflake/ADF migration weighs a pending offer against a comfortable current role, with much of the thread turning on stack ownership after contractors handled the migration. *Why it matters:* the comments double as a read on how teams value modernisation experience right now.
